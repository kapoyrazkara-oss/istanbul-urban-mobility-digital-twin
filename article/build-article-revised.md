# From Research to Simulation: Building an Adaptive Traffic Signal System for Istanbul

## TL;DR

Can adaptive traffic signals reduce waiting time under different traffic-demand patterns? I built a SUMO- and Python-based prototype that compares a fixed-time controller with a traffic-responsive controller on the same network and route demand. In the initial single-run experiments, adaptive control reduced average waiting time by **24.7%** in the balanced scenario and **35.7%** in the north-south-heavy scenario; I then ran five matched random seeds per scenario to test whether those improvements remain consistent rather than relying on one run.

## Research question

Can a simple, interpretable adaptive traffic-signal controller reduce vehicle waiting time compared with a fixed-time baseline under more than one traffic-demand pattern?

The goal is not to claim that this prototype is ready for deployment in Istanbul. The goal is to build a small, measurable pilot that exposes the design trade-offs before a real intersection is considered.

## What I built

The prototype uses Eclipse SUMO for microscopic traffic simulation and Python/TraCI for signal control. It contains a four-way signalised intersection for the original experiment and a larger 5×5 network for the digital-twin replay. The reproducible article experiment uses two route-demand patterns:

- **Balanced:** traffic is distributed across the approaches.
- **North-south-heavy:** the north-south approaches receive heavier demand.

For each scenario, the same demand seed is used for both controllers.

## Controller design

The fixed-time baseline follows the signal program already defined in the SUMO network. The adaptive controller is rule-based and deliberately interpretable:

1. Measure stopped vehicles on the incoming approaches.
2. Compare the active approach queue with the opposing approach queue.
3. When the active direction has more pressure, extend the current green phase.
4. Keep the extension bounded so that no direction can hold the signal indefinitely.

The controller is not a machine-learning model. This is intentional: the first build is designed to make the cause of each decision visible and reproducible. The exact minimum/maximum phase values, extension step, and demand rates are documented in the repository and are not tuned after looking at the final result.

## Initial single-run result

The first article experiment produced the following results:

| Scenario | Fixed-time waiting | Adaptive waiting | Initial change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Balanced | 12.97 s | 9.76 s | 24.7% lower |
| North-south-heavy | 15.62 s | 10.05 s | 35.7% lower |

These values came from one run per controller and scenario. They are therefore preliminary, not a general claim about all traffic conditions.

## Multi-seed validation

To test repeatability, I added a script that runs five matched seeds across both scenarios and both controllers. The paired design is important: for a given seed, the fixed-time and adaptive controllers see the same generated demand.

The aggregated table below is generated by `experiments/aggregate_results.py` after running `experiments/run_all.py`:

| Scenario | Controller | n | Mean waiting time | Standard deviation | Improvement |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Balanced | Fixed-time | 5 | 12.93 ± 0.31 s | 0.31 s | — |
| Balanced | Adaptive | 5 | 10.52 ± 0.24 s | 0.24 s | 18.64% lower |
| North-south-heavy | Fixed-time | 5 | 14.74 ± 0.30 s | 0.30 s | — |
| North-south-heavy | Adaptive | 5 | 10.04 ± 0.47 s | 0.47 s | 31.89% lower |

The multi-seed results are lower than the initial single-run percentages, which is exactly why the validation step was necessary. The revised claim is that adaptive control reduced mean waiting time by 18.64% in the balanced scenario and 31.89% in the north-south-heavy scenario across five matched seeds.

## What I learned

The first implementation made the controller easy to run, but the review showed that “it ran once” is not enough evidence. The main improvement is therefore not another visual feature; it is the experimental protocol: matched seeds, multiple repetitions, explicit parameters, and an aggregation script. The initial 24.7% and 35.7% results did not survive unchanged, but the direction of the effect did.

## Limitations

This is a rule-based prototype on a small, synthetic network. It does not use live Istanbul detector data, does not reproduce a geospatially exact Istanbul corridor, and does not model every safety, pedestrian, weather, or public-transport constraint. The results should be interpreted as a simulation finding under the stated assumptions, not as a prediction of city-wide impact.
